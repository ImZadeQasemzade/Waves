Waves · fields of a moving charge

Every electromagnetic wave starts the same way: a charge accelerates, and the "news" of its motion ripples outward at the speed of light. Drag the charge around — then flip on oscillate and watch kinks peel off the field lines as radiation. That peeling is the wave your antenna makes.

What you're seeing

Each line is an electric field line of a single point charge, drawn for this exact instant. The field at every point is computed from the Liénard–Wiechert fields — the exact relativistic field of an arbitrarily moving charge — with the retarded time solved numerically at every grid point. Nothing is faked: the lag, the compression ahead of a fast charge, and the radiation kinks all fall out of Maxwell's equations.

  • Drag the charge. Nearby field lines follow it almost instantly; far ones still point at where it was. The boundary between those regions expands at c — lower the light-speed slider and you can watch the "news" travel.
  • Whip it around. Every kink you put into a field line is a transverse disturbance propagating outward. Accelerating charge radiates — this is the mechanism, not an analogy.
  • Oscillate. The charge becomes a tiny dipole antenna. Close in, the field is a mess of near-field terms; a few wavelengths out, the kinks organize into clean transverse waves marching outward at c. The magnetic field (not drawn) circles the oscillation axis, perpendicular to both the E-lines and the direction of travel.

Why this matters for antennas

A radio antenna is just this, engineered: drive electrons back and forth in a wire and each oscillation sheds a wave. The far-field pattern — the familiar dipole "donut" — is what those radiated kinks add up to.
